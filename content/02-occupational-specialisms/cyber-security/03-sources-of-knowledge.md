# Cyber Security - Discover, Evaluate and Apply Reliable Sources of Knowledge

*Digital Support and Security T Level → Occupational Specialisms → Cyber Security → Sources of Knowledge*

This is Content Area 3 of the Cyber Security specialism. It mirrors the equivalent content area in the other three specialisms, but is populated with sources and bodies specific to the cyber security profession - professional bodies, accreditation schemes and vulnerability databases a working cyber security technician would actually use.

## 3.1 Sources of knowledge applicable to cyber security

Academic publications (textbooks, journals, periodicals), supplier literature (e.g. Microsoft, AWS), websites (wikis, forums, community encyclopaedias, manufacturer sites, Q&A sites), webinars, social media (company profiles on X/Facebook/LinkedIn), blogs (vulnerability discussion), vlogs (mitigation-strategy tutorials), professional networks (conferences/events), professional bodies (CIISec, CREST, ISACA, UK Cyber Security Council), e-learning (MOOCs), peers, cyber security policies/procedures (e.g. an information security policy), guidelines/legislation (e.g. DPA 2018), regulating authorities (e.g. ICO), industry standards (NCSC Cyber Essentials, CIS Benchmarks), industry accreditation (CompTIA, Certified Cyber Professional, (ISC)²) and databases (e.g. CVE).

**Skill**: identify the purpose/parameters of a topic or scenario, then identify three appropriate sources of knowledge to support it. *(M5, D3, D5.)*

## 3.2 Reliability and validity factors

Credibility of the publisher (affiliation to recognised bodies, reputation, relevant experience/qualifications, industry-certified accreditation), support from credible citations, relevance to context, currency of publication (version number, date), and absence of bias (opinion hasn't shaped the source). **Skill**: recommend and justify which sources to trust, and whether more sources are needed.

## 3.3 Searching for information

Identify the requirements of the topic/scenario → search sources and extract relevant information (e.g. on threat intelligence, common attack techniques, security policy/procedure, relevant guidelines/legislation/standards, or evolving cyber issues). *(M5, D1, D6.)*

## 3.4 Bias in a cyber security context

Author/proprietary bias, confirmation bias, selection bias, cultural bias, and **availability bias** (an opinion skewed toward the most recent or memorable experience). **Skill**: identify the type of bias present and its indicators, and explain clearly how it arose and how it could be avoided.

## 3.5 Evaluation techniques and tools

**Techniques**: triangulation (cross-checking across more than two sources for consistency), formative evaluation (improving a task while it's in progress), summative evaluation (reviewing achievement at the end), observation (real-time review), corroboration (strengthening a finding via cross-referencing), drawing conclusions, and making recommendations (e.g. for information security training). **Tools**: gap analysis (current state vs. desired state), maturity assessments (an organisation's ability to hit predictable outcomes), user diaries (ongoing, timely documentation of a process).

## 3.6 Critical thinking to support objective evaluation

Identify relevant information (arguments, views, opinions) → analyse it (bias, objectivity, links between data) → select relevant techniques/tools → evaluate findings → draw conclusions. **Skill**: apply this end-to-end, then select and apply the right techniques/tools to a defined evaluation requirement and record the outcome. *(E1, E3, E4, E6, M1, M2, M3, M8, M10, D4.)*

## 3.7 Communication methods for sharing cyber security knowledge

**Digital services** (two-way channels: helpdesk, phone, email, SMS, chat), **social media channels** (organisational, public, community, personal), **knowledge bases/management systems** - including sector-specific ones such as **CyBOK** (the Cyber Security Body of Knowledge) and **MITRE ATT&CK** (a knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques), plus wikis, blogs, training platforms and vendor subscriptions - and **project management tools** (issue logs, Gantt charts, Kanban boards, burndown charts).

## 3.8 Cyber security impacts on critical national infrastructure

Disruption to supply chains (food, raw materials); utilities - energy (power cuts, surges, under-voltage, gas supply loss), water/sanitation (loss of supply, flooding, treatment disruption), government (communication/policy interruption), finance (failed payments, transfer interruption), healthcare (compromised patient records, impaired communication/treatment), communications/ISPs (service loss, eavesdropping, impersonation), defence (impaired military/defence capability), and transport (disrupted public/private transport and emergency dispatch).

## 3.9 Purpose and types of control systems

Purpose: receive data from remote sensors, measure values, and control a process or asset across locations. Types: industrial control systems (critical national infrastructure), medical control systems (life-sustaining equipment, patient data), facility-related control systems (e.g. door locks), automotive control systems (e.g. engine/fuel systems).

## 3.10 Evolving IoT cyber security risks

Poor data-protection controls (insufficient built-in security, too low-powered for encryption, shared-network exposure), poor password protection (weak/default factory passwords), insecure data transfer/storage (unencrypted data in processing/transit/rest) and weak security-update mechanisms (unvalidated firmware, insecure delivery, no anti-rollback, no update notifications).

## 3.11 Importance of information assurance and governance (IAG)

IAG guides the development and improvement of security strategies/policies/processes, supports auditing them, informs their maintenance through a compliance monitoring plan, and provides confirmation of compliance (e.g. against ISO standards or the NIST cyber framework).

## 3.12 Comparing sources and recommending action

Compare sources using reliability/validity factors (publisher credibility, currency) → recommend and justify which source(s) to use, and whether additional sources are needed. *(M5, M6, D5.)*

## Key terms

- **CyBOK** - Cyber Security Body of Knowledge, a structured foundation of cyber security knowledge areas.
- **MITRE ATT&CK** - a knowledge base cataloguing real-world adversary tactics and techniques, widely used as a reference in threat analysis.
- **Availability bias** - judging based disproportionately on the most recent or memorable experience.
- **IAG** - Information Assurance and Governance, the discipline of guiding, auditing and maintaining an organisation's security posture.
